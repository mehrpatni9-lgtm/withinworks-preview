Why transformation starts from the inside out

By Megha Bhalla, CPCC

People often come to me wanting to change something external. A team that isn’t performing. A title they haven’t been given yet. A career that no longer fits the person living it. And almost every time, within the first few sessions, we end up somewhere else entirely: not at the goal, but at the lens through which they’re looking at the goal.

This isn’t a detour. It’s the work.

I’ve spent over 25 years in rooms where people were trying to force change from the outside in. New structures, new strategies, new targets. Some of it worked, for a while. But the leaders and professionals who actually transformed, who came out the other side of a merger or a career break or a promotion genuinely different, weren’t the ones who pushed hardest against their circumstances. They were the ones who got curious about themselves first.

Here’s the belief at the center of my work: people don’t experience reality directly. They experience reality through identity, memory, and perspective. Two people can sit in the same restructuring meeting and walk out with entirely different internal experiences: one energized by the challenge, the other quietly convinced they’re about to be found out. The meeting was identical. The lens wasn’t.

This is why so much external change doesn’t stick. You can redesign an org chart, but if the person leading it still sees themselves through an old story (not strategic enough, too soft, too late to the table), the redesign will eventually bend back toward that story. You can leave a job, take the sabbatical, start the business, but if the underlying identity hasn’t shifted, the new chapter tends to rhyme uncomfortably with the old one.

I want to be clear about what I’m not saying. I’m not saying circumstances don’t matter, or that mindset alone solves structural problems. Growth, restructuring, re-entry after time away: these are real, concrete situations with real stakes. What I’m saying is that how a person meets those situations, what they believe is possible, what they think they’re allowed to want, is shaped by something deeper than the situation itself.

This is where the “inside out” language comes from, and I want to be precise about what it means, because it’s easy to hear it as something soft or vague. It isn’t about fixing what’s wrong with you. It isn’t about forcing positivity or chasing a better version of yourself through sheer will. It’s about noticing the internal lens you’ve been using, often without realizing you had a choice in the matter, and getting curious about whether it’s still serving you.

In practice, this looks like slowing down enough to ask better questions. Not “what should I do next” but “what am I believing right now that’s shaping what I think I should do next.” Not “how do I get people to follow me” but “what version of myself shows up when I feel unsure of my authority, and where did that version come from.” These aren’t therapy questions. They’re leadership and life questions, and in my experience, they’re the ones that actually move something.

I saw this firsthand building the people function at Traveazy from the ground up, through a pandemic and two mergers, growing the organization past 350 people. The technical work was demanding. But what determined whether people thrived through that much change wasn’t the org design or the process documentation. It was whether they had done the internal work to meet uncertainty without collapsing into old patterns of fear or control.

I don’t give people a formula for this, because there isn’t one that holds up across different lives. What I do is create the space for someone to actually look at their own lens, often for the first time in years, and decide consciously what they want to keep and what they’re ready to put down. The external change, the new role, the rebuilt confidence, the clearer boundaries, tends to follow. Not because it was forced, but because it finally has somewhere real to stand.

That’s the whole premise of this work. Not forcing external change. Not chasing goals for their own sake. Not fixing what’s “wrong” with you, because I don’t believe there’s something broken that needs fixing. Just changing the internal lens through which you experience your own life, and trusting that everything else has a way of reorganizing itself around that shift.

If this resonates, the next step is a conversation.
